I am only seven years old when I first understand what it is that my father fears.

We are standing on the deserted pasture near the southern slope of Buck’s Peak. The wind is fierce, whipping my hair across my face and into my mouth. Mother is gesturing toward the peak. “That’s where the school is,” she says.

My father grunts. “I don’t like it,” he says. “The government’s got no business telling me how to raise my kids.”

This is the first I have heard of school. I know other kids go—Jentel down the road, the ones at church—but it has never occurred to me that I might go too.

The wind picks up, howling through the juniper trees. My father stares at the mountain for a long moment, then turns and walks back to the house. We follow him, our feet crunching on the frozen grass.

That was the beginning of my father’s great fear of the government, and of the world beyond the mountain. It was a fear that would shape our lives, a fear that would keep us children trapped in the shadow of the peak, a fear that I would spend my youth trying to escape, and my adulthood trying to understand.
